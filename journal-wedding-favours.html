How many favours, and what goes in them

Ordering by guest count rather than by guess — a working method for the couple who has three hundred people to thank and six weeks to do it in.

Start with households, not heads

The single most common error is ordering one favour per invitee. Favours are almost always taken home per household, not per person — a couple attending together will leave with one box between them, not two. Counting heads can inflate an order by a third.

Work from your seating list instead. Count households, then add ten per cent. That margin covers late additions, the two that get damaged in transit, and the ones your mother gives away on the night to people who were never on the list.

What actually belongs inside

A favour is carried through a long evening, put in a car, and opened at home two days later. That journey rules out anything fragile, anything that melts, and anything that needs refrigeration. It also rules out most things that photograph beautifully on a table.

What survives — and what people keep — falls into four categories, and a good favour has one of each:

  • Something to burn. A candle is the item most often reported as used.
  • Something to drink. Tea travels better than coffee and suits more people.
  • Something sweet, sealed. Chocolate with a high cocoa content holds up in Indian heat far better than milk chocolate.
  • Something that stays out. A small ceramic dish or a keepsake tin outlives everything else in the box.

Resist the urge to add a fifth thing. A box with four considered items reads as generous; the same box with seven reads as filled.

When to order

Four to eight weeks before the function, and at the longer end if your wedding falls in peak season between October and February. Printing on ribbon and cards adds a week, and artwork approval usually takes longer than anyone plans for because it needs two people to agree.

A useful sequence: confirm the guest list at eight weeks, approve a sample at six, and take delivery two days before the function. Never schedule delivery for the morning of.

On personalising them

Names and a date on the ribbon is enough. A long printed message on a card is read once and then becomes something the recipient feels guilty about throwing away. If you want to say more, write it — a short line in your own hand on a blank card beats three printed sentences.

In short: count households and add ten per cent, keep it to four items, and order four to eight weeks out.
